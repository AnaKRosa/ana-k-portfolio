You have a website. Maybe you built it yourself watching tutorials, maybe you hired someone, maybe you used whatever builder made sense to you at the time. It's live. And... not much is happening. It just sits there, and you're not sure what to do to make it actually work for you.

Good news: you don't need a redesign or a new budget to start moving the needle. Let's go through a practical, free self-audit you can run yourself — or hand to your designer, developer, or whoever manages your website's edits.

Here's the order I'd tackle it in, from most important to least.

<h2 id="s1">1. Define what you actually want your website to do</h2>

Before touching anything, answer this: what is the one thing you want your website to accomplish? Get calls booked? Sell a product? Collect leads? Build credibility so people trust you before they reach out?

Everything else on this list depends on this answer. You can't fix a website's clarity or its call-to-action if you don't know what you're optimizing it for in the first place.

<h2 id="s2">2. Run the 5-second hero test</h2>

Once you know your goal, look at your hero section — the first thing people see, no scrolling.

Show it to someone who has no idea what your business does. Give them five seconds. Then ask: "Based only on what you just saw, what do I do, and who is it for?"

If they get it right away — great, leave it alone. If they hesitate, guess wrong, or take too long to answer, that's your sign: simplify your headline until anyone can understand what you do and who it's for in a few seconds, no scrolling required.

<h2 id="s3">3. Give people exactly one thing to do</h2>

Once a visitor knows what you do and who it's for, the next question is: what do you want them to do about it?

I see this all the time — a client feels like every single CTA is important, so they end up wanting three, sometimes four, all fighting for attention on the same page. Book a call, sign up for the newsletter, download the guide, check the shop. The problem is, when everything is "important," nothing is. The visitor gets confused about what to actually do, and confusion usually means they do nothing at all.

Pick one. Call you? Book an appointment? Watch a video? Whatever it is, put it front and center, above the fold, in your own words. One clear next step beats three good ones, every time.

<h2 id="s4">4. Check your navigation</h2>

Is everything a potential client needs easy to find, fast? Is anything missing that they'd expect to see? Is anything there that's just clutter, that nobody actually clicks?

Good navigation isn't about having more — it's about having exactly what someone needs to make a decision, with nothing in the way.

<h2 id="s5">5. Run a mobile check</h2>

Let's be honest — we're all glued to our phones right now. There's a good chance more than 80% of your potential clients are seeing your website on a phone screen first, and you can't afford for it to not be optimized for that.

Start with your own phone. Open your site and actually look at it, section by section. Then borrow your brother's phone, your best friend's, anyone with a different device, and check it there too. You'd think it would look the same everywhere — but it doesn't always. Something that looks perfect on an iPhone can shift, overlap, or break on an Android, or the other way around.

<h2 id="s6">6. Run a free speed test</h2>

A slow website quietly kills conversions before anyone even reads your copy. Tools like Google PageSpeed Insights or GTmetrix are free and take two minutes. If your site is loading slowly, that alone could be costing you more visitors than any copy problem.

<h2 id="s7">7. Name your services clearly and briefly</h2>

List your services in a way that's fast to scan. Nobody wants to read three paragraphs to figure out what you offer. Be specific, be brief, and make sure someone can understand what you do for them in a glance — not a study session.

<h2 id="s8">8. Be upfront about pricing (or at least give a range)</h2>

Does your potential client know whether they can even afford you? If you sell a product or a service, showing pricing — or at least a starting range — removes one of the biggest hesitations before someone reaches out.

<blockquote>
<p>It's better to get one good lead who has the right budget and is genuinely ready to move forward, than a thousand bad leads from people who were never going to be able to pay you in the first place.</p>
</blockquote>

Pricing isn't just information, it's a filter. It saves you time, saves them time, and means the people who do reach out are actually a fit.

<h2 id="s9">9. Add an FAQ section</h2>

Answer the questions clients always ask before they even ask them: refunds, guarantees, how payment works, turnaround time, whatever comes up again and again in your inbox or DMs. Every question you answer on your site is one less email you have to write, and one less hesitation your visitor has to overcome before contacting you.

<h2 id="s10">10. Make it easy to reach you</h2>

Don't complicate your client's life by making them scroll all the way down to the footer just to hunt for your email or phone number. If you can, make it effortless: a permanent CTA with a tap-to-call phone link, a booking link (something like Calendly or Cal.com), or whatever works for your business — the tool itself matters less than the result. The point is that reaching you should take one tap, not a treasure hunt.

<h2 id="s11">11. Add basic trust signals</h2>

Testimonials, client logos, reviews, certifications, social media links — anything that tells a stranger "other real people trust this business." This is one of the cheapest, fastest ways to boost credibility, and most sites either skip it or bury it too far down the page.

<h2 id="s12">12. Check your meta description (yes, that little text under your link on Google)</h2>

Do you know what a meta description is? It's that short block of text you see under a website's name when you search something on Google — the name on top, and right below it, a little description explaining what you'll find if you click.

That name should say the correct name of your business or service and which page it belongs to. And that description should actually tell someone what's waiting for them on the other side. This costs nothing to fix, and it's one of the first things search engines — and increasingly, AI search tools — read to understand your business before anyone even lands on your site.

<h2 id="s13">13. Make sure you have analytics installed</h2>

You can't fix what you can't measure. If you don't already have Google Analytics or something similar connected, set it up. It's free, and it's the only way you'll know if any of the changes above are actually working.

<h2 id="s14">The bottom line</h2>

None of this requires a redesign, a developer, or a single dollar. Just fifteen minutes, a critical eye, and the willingness to look at your own website the way a stranger would. Start from the top of this list, work your way down, and you'll be surprised how much a handful of free changes can do.
